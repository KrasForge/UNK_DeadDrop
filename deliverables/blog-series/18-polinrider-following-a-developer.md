In early November I was checking where copies of one UNK_DeadDrop take-home assessment had gone, and one of the developers concerned had also published a second, unrelated recruitment exercise: a copy-trading project whose backend and frontend start scripts both ran an extra file called `init` before the application itself. That file was not part of the operation I had spent four months documenting. It was an obfuscated Node loader that read the address of its next server from the recipient field of an Ethereum transaction, and when I recovered it statically, resolving all 372 of its strings and all 538 of its decoder call sites without ever running it, nothing in it crossed to the UNK_DeadDrop operator. This post and the five that follow describe where that loader led, and the first thing to explain is why I treat what I found as a separate operation, called PolinRider in public reporting, and not as another branch of the case that the earlier parts of this series and [Part 17](17-two-operations-one-ecosystem.md) describe.

## An extra step before the application starts

The assessment came from a recruiter persona called Hiring777, seeded from a GitHub account created on 14 May 2026 under the address `christoper929@outlook[.]com`. From mid-May that account published clean base versions of a copy-trading exercise and several related ones, with names such as `copy-trading-test`, `vaultpay-home-task`, `react-uiux-dev-test` and `smart-contract-engineer-role`. The affected copy prepended `node init` to its ordinary development, build and start commands in all three of its package manifests, and placed `init`, `backend/init` and `frontend/init` beside them, so a candidate who simply followed the instructions reached the loader while doing exactly what the exercise asked. Because the danger was in one modified copy and not necessarily in the assignment as a whole, I read every public repository matching the seven assessment brands across 25 accounts, the recruiter's own among them, checking each file tree for an extensionless `init` and each `package.json` for the prepended command.

That search produced a narrower result than I expected. About nineteen of those accounts belong to candidates who hold 26 matching copies between them, one of them probably unrelated, and with the recruiter's two base copies that makes 28 repositories, of which exactly one carries the dropper. The recruiter's own copies and every other candidate copy pushed between May and October were clean, which matters in both directions when it comes to notification: the other candidates should not be told that their copies are infected, and the one affected developer's exposure dates from whenever they last ran a development, build or start script after the loader entered the submission. The infection also came through the repository rather than through a dependency, since none of the 47 packages on a public toolkit list for this campaign appears in the 152 manifests and lockfiles in that developer's account. The obvious strings turned out to be useless for finding anyone else, because `node init` followed by a watcher or a compiler is ordinary, the loader's deobfuscated array identifier returns 706 code-search hits that are mostly linter configurations, and its stage-two path fragment returns 838 hits dominated by advisories about this very family.

## A campaign that already had a name

Public reporting had already named what the loader belongs to. OpenSourceMalware's reports of [March](https://opensourcemalware.com/blog/polinrider-dprk-compromised-hundreds-of-github-repos), [April](https://opensourcemalware.com/blog/polinrider-rides-again-north-korean-attack-expands-across-github) and [July 2026](https://opensourcemalware.com/blog/polinrider-blast-radius-grows) describe PolinRider as a campaign of obfuscated loaders injected into developer repositories, first through configuration files in compromised npm packages and later, as their [developer guide](https://opensourcemalware.com/blog/developer-guide-getting-over-polinrider) documents, through a workspace task. The same researchers named [NullReceiver](https://github.com/OpenSourceMalware/NullReceiver), the technique of encoding an IPv4 address in the recipient of an empty Ethereum transfer, and published one resolver wallet and one host. [GitGuardian](https://blog.gitguardian.com/ghostaction-x-polinrider/) described the multi-stage payload chain, and in November [JFrog](https://research.jfrog.com/post/accurasofthire-fake-interview/), [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/accurasofthire-fake-interview-developer-machines) and [Sonatype](https://www.sonatype.com/blog/polinrider-loader-reappears-in-hijacked-npm-packages) reported npm campaigns that read the same resolver wallets. In that vocabulary PolinRider is the campaign, NullReceiver is the resolver technique of its Ethereum branch, and the file in the Hiring777 copy is a PolinRider loader delivered through a take-home exercise, an entry route that secondary reporting already lists among the campaign's methods.

That leaves a clear line between prior work and my own. The campaign's name, the existence of its resolver branches, the later stealer stages and the attribution all come from those publications, and I use them as such. What I added is the part that was not yet public when I started: a third resolver wallet that appears in no report I have found, the closed enumeration of all three wallets with the shared hosts that tie them together, the money that pays for the resolvers, the loader generations of a second blockchain branch, and the way the kit moves from one infected workstation into every repository it can reach. Parts 2 to 6 follow those threads in order, and [Part 7](24-polinrider-money.md) follows the money in depth. Where a step in them rests on my inference rather than on a record, I say so at that step, because a reader who has to separate an observation from a conclusion should not have to reconstruct the distinction from a footnote.

## Why PolinRider is a separate operation

The two operations differ at every layer an operator has to build. UNK_DeadDrop reaches developers through ClickFix pages, a malicious editor extension and repositories that run on folder open, and its payload reads its server configuration from a GitLab dead drop; PolinRider hides obfuscated JavaScript loaders in repositories as an extensionless `init` or a fake font file, starts them from an npm script or a hidden editor task, and reads its server address from blockchain records. UNK_DeadDrop's lure and server estate sat on Advin Services from July to November 2026, while PolinRider's resolver hosts sat on Evoxt's AS149440 and on AS399486. When I compared them I found no shared certificate, panel fingerprint, address, subnet or autonomous system. A path analysis over my findings ledger found no independent observational link at all, since the only two paths between the blocks run through a generic string-array obfuscation rule and a bare attribution tag, neither of which is an observation, and community detection over 2,218 observables placed every connected PolinRider, NullReceiver and Hiring777 record in communities that hold no UNK_DeadDrop record.

What the two do share is the ecosystem and a few victims, and neither is a link. Two developers answered both fake recruiters, which is unsurprising when two crews approach the same small population of crypto-focused freelance developers, since someone who answers one fake recruiter is by that fact more likely to answer another. One detail shows how easily a shared victim produces a false connection. The affected developer's account holds a fork of an UNK_DeadDrop take-home lure, and read naively that fork shows an UNK_DeadDrop identity authoring a PolinRider commit; in fact its folder-open task is the lure's original weaponization inherited from upstream, and PolinRider's worm amends commits in every working copy it reaches under whoever authored them. The repository joins the two operations only through the victim's disk, which is the kind of overlap I decline throughout this series, together with the shared folder-open technique and the BeaverTail, OtterCookie and InvisibleFerret family records that belong to the wider ecosystem rather than to either operation.

Attribution follows the same discipline. OpenSourceMalware attributes PolinRider to North Korea and describes it as a Lazarus Group contributor, and [Socket](https://socket.dev/blog/polinrider-north-korea-linked-supply-chain-campaign-expands) places it in the Contagious Interview, or Famous Chollima, developer-targeting campaign, which [MITRE tracks as G1052](https://attack.mitre.org/groups/G1052/); those are the publishers' assessments, not mine. What I can add is that Hiring777's resolver wallet shares two hosts, the same marker, the same staging paths and the same RPC list with the wallet named in OpenSourceMalware's reporting, which makes Hiring777 a live node of a reported campaign rather than an unknown actor, without showing that it is the same crew. Google's [EtherHiding report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/dprk-adopts-etherhiding) attributes the technique and the JADESNOW downloader to a cluster it calls UNC5342 and never mentions PolinRider, so it supports a statement about UNC5342 and nothing about this campaign, and naming that cluster for the loader I hold would be one inferential step further than the evidence goes.

## A date that does not fit

The assessment also carries a chronology I cannot close. The commit that adds the loader carries an unsigned author date in early June, while the Ethereum wallet the loader hard-codes received its first gas on BNB Smart Chain on 3 July and sent its first resolver transaction on 4 September. Those dates fit a dormant implant sitting in the repository with a wallet that was generated in advance and switched on later, and they equally fit a commit date that is simply misleading, because, as [Part 4](21-polinrider-repository-spread.md) shows, this kit is written into repositories by amending existing commits whose author dates it inherits. The public repository cannot decide between those readings, and I do not rank them. The developer's own account of when the files were received and run would settle it, and until then I neither date the infection from that commit nor describe the gap as a confirmed dormant period.

The loader itself gave me firmer ground than its commit did, because its recovered strings described exactly where it would look for a server, and following that lookup turned an obfuscated file into a public, dated record of the operator's infrastructure. [Part 2](19-polinrider-ethereum-address-book.md) explains how the Ethereum branch uses a transaction's recipient field as an address book, and how the campaign's older TRON and BNB Smart Chain branch does the same with payloads.
